Maverick Wall · identity

Five marks and a wordmark

A clean-sheet identity: nothing here descends from the tile currently shipping on the add-on. Five marks, drawn in the product's own geometry and its own colours, plus a wordmark drawn from scratch so the logo owes nothing to a font that has to be installed. Every one is shown on the Board and Paper Almanac themes, in flat monochrome, and at the sizes it will really be used at.

01

Panel

Draws the hardware

The thing itself: a portrait screen, lit, with a calendar on it.

Why it works

It names the category in one look — this is a wall display, not another web app you open when you remember to. The portrait ratio is the product's real default (1080×1920), and nothing else in this category has a portrait icon, so it stands out in a row of square ones.

Where it breaks

Small, it reads as a phone — a lit portrait rectangle with rounded corners is a phone, and this is the one product that must not be mistaken for something you hold. The outline is the second problem: a 3.5-unit stroke on a 64 grid is sub-pixel at 16 px, so the favicon needs a filled redraw, which makes it two marks rather than one.

Seven

Draws a week

Seven strokes, and the one you are standing in. The week, without drawing a calendar.

Why it works

It is the only mark in the set built on rhythm rather than a grid, which makes it the most distinct silhouette of the five — you can pick it out of a sidebar without resolving any detail. It reduces better than anything except 05, because vertical strokes stay vertical strokes at any size, and the tall amber one carries the whole idea on height alone, so monochrome costs nothing.

Where it breaks

Seven bars with a tall middle is also what an audio equaliser looks like, and a media-player reading is a real risk on a device that hangs on a wall next to a television. The count is doing the work here, and nobody counts an icon — so it says "a week" only to somebody who has already been told.

03

Lit Cell

Draws the idea

A month, quiet. One cell lit. The only mark here that says what the product is for rather than what it looks like.

Why it works

Seven columns, because the wall's month grid is seven columns — the mark is the product's real geometry rather than a drawing of it. And it behaves the way the wall behaves: the field falls away to texture, the amber cell is the last thing standing, and what survives the reduction is exactly what somebody walking past is meant to read.

The contrast is opacity rather than hue, so monochrome needs no special handling and one-colour print, a social card and a stencil all come off the same file.

Where it breaks

Thirty-five cells is too many below about 20 px: the field turns to grey mush and only the amber square reads. That needs the 5×4 redraw (lit-cell-small.svg), which is a second file to keep in step. And a grid of squares is not an unheard-of shape — the ownership is entirely in the single lit cell, so the amber must never be dropped to make a "simplified" version.

04

Out of Line

Draws the name

A maverick is the one that will not run with the herd. One cell steps out of rank, and its slot is left empty behind it.

Why it works

It is the only option that earns the word Maverick rather than working around it, and it explains itself in a sentence to somebody standing in a kitchen. The half-cell horizontal offset is doing real work: aligned to the column it left, the displacement reads as a missing row; offset, it reads as a decision.

Where it breaks

This project's ninth rule is that the wall never looks broken — and here is a mark whose whole subject is a cell out of alignment. At 24 px and below, the empty slot and the escaped cell stop reading as a story and start reading as a rendering fault or a half-loaded image. It is the cleverest of the five and the one most likely to be quietly mistaken for a bug.

05

Corner

Draws nothing

Two shapes: the wall, and today. The calendar reduced until only the relationship is left.

Why it works

The most trademarkable of the five and the one that will age best, because it depicts no interface that can go out of date. It also holds its shape further down than anything else here — two solid forms with four units of clear space between them still read as two forms at 16 px. On a 512 tile it has presence with no detail to fuss over.

Where it breaks

It says nothing about calendars. It bought its legibility with meaning, so without the wordmark beside it, it is a shape — which makes it the option most dependent on the lockup. The notched square also carries a faint whiff of a file manager.

The wordmark

Set in Oswald 700 and outlined to paths at build time, so the committed file needs no font installed anywhere and cannot silently fall back to Arial — but the letterforms are a type designer's, not ours. Oswald is already bundled in the image for the display themes (SIL Open Font License 1.1), so the wordmark adds no new file, no new licence and nothing to download. It is the only face in that set drawn for signage rather than for paragraphs, which is what a name on a wall is, and being condensed it sits beside a wide grid mark without crowding it.

Three rounds of this were drawn by hand first, and all three came back “cut off”. The first chamfered every curve at 45°; the second fixed the curves but left an M too narrow to hold a counter; the third fixed the M and the whole thing still read as messy, because a display alphabet drawn on the way to something else is a worse alphabet than one somebody spent a year on. The honest lesson is the same one the QR encoder taught: draw the thing the product actually needs, and use somebody else's work for the thing that is already solved.

The lockups

Shown with 03. In both, the mark is scaled so its ink height matches the cap height — dropped in at its icon size the grid reads a size smaller than the type beside it, which is the commonest way a lockup goes subtly wrong.

The recommendation

03, Lit Cell, with the wordmark. It is the only one of the five that says what the product is for rather than what it looks like, and it is built from the wall's own seven-column grid, so it stays true after the next redesign of the interface it is not depicting. Its reduction behaviour is the argument: the month becomes texture, today stays lit. That is the product.

05, Corner is the alternate worth arguing for. It is the better trademark and it is the one that survives smallest; it just needs the wordmark doing more of the work, which the lockup now does.

04 is the one to reach for if the name should do the talking, but not before somebody has looked at it at 16 px in a real tab and decided it does not look broken. 02 is the wildcard — the most distinct silhouette here, and the one most likely to be read as a music player.
